## Hook (0:00)

[VISUAL: a calendar filling up until it's solid color]

Busy all day, and nothing that matters moved? Greg McKeown's book Essentialism says the fix isn't better time management. It's doing fewer things, on purpose. Here's what the book teaches, what holds up, and what research says works even better. [Source Brief, thesis]

## Part 1: What the book teaches (0:25)

[VISUAL: two large titles appear: "The mindset" and "The method"]

The book has two big ideas: a mindset, and a three-step method. [Source Card, structure]

### Topic 1: The Essentialist mindset

[VISUAL: three cards flip in, one per point]

First, you always have a choice. McKeown treats choosing as something you do, not something you have. When we forget that, other people's priorities fill our day. [Ch. 2]

Second, only a few things are vital. Most of what lands on your desk is trivial. He ties this to Joseph Juran, the quality pioneer, who observed that fixing a small share of problems can improve quality enormously. [C-02]

Third, every yes is a trade-off. Instead of asking "how can I do both?", ask "which problem do I want?" [Ch. 4]

### Topic 2: The three-step method

[VISUAL: whiteboard draws Explore, Eliminate, Execute with arrows]

Step one, Explore. Make space to think, protect your sleep, and filter every option with strict criteria. His example: in a well-known study of violin students, the best ones slept about 8.6 hours a day. [Ch. 5 to 9, C-04]

Step two, Eliminate. Pick one clear goal, learn to say a graceful no, and cut your losses on commitments that aren't working, even when you've already invested a lot. [Ch. 10 to 14, C-10]

Step three, Execute. Add buffer time, build on small wins, and turn your most important work into routine, so it happens almost on autopilot. [Ch. 15 to 20, C-12, C-13, C-14]

## Part 2: Does it hold up? (2:40)

[VISUAL: a green check stamp over "Buffer", "Sleep", "Cut losses", "Routine"]

The short version: the practical tools hold up. We really do underestimate how long tasks take. In one classic study, students expected to finish their thesis in about 34 days, and took about 55. [C-12] Losing sleep clearly hurts attention and working memory. [C-06] We do keep investing in losing projects because of what we've already spent. [C-10] And routines do become automatic, though it takes closer to two months than 21 days. [C-14]

[VISUAL: a small grey label "author's experience"]

The big framing ideas, like his "clarity paradox," come from the author's experience, not from research. Useful, but not proven. [C-01, C-09]

## Part 3: What research says works better (3:40)

[VISUAL: split screen, "The book says" on the left, "Research shows" on the right]

For three of the book's tips, the evidence points to something more reliable. Here's the swap for each.

**Swap 1: from the 90 Percent Rule to if-then plans.**
The book says: score every option from 0 to 100, and if it's under 90, say no. We found no study that tests that rule. [C-08]
What research shows: if-then plans work. Write them down in advance: "If a new request comes in, then I ask what it replaces." A meta-analysis of 94 studies found these plans help people follow through on goals, and even help them drop commitments that are failing. [S-01]

[VISUAL: a sticky note reading "If a new request comes in, then I ask what it replaces"]

**Swap 2: from play to real breaks.**
The book says play sharpens your thinking. In adults, that evidence is thin. [C-07]
What research shows: short breaks during work reliably raise your energy and cut fatigue. And after demanding work, a longer break, over 10 minutes, may be what you need. [S-02]

[VISUAL: a timer counting a 10-minute break]

**Swap 3: from small wins to tracking progress.**
The book says small wins are the most powerful motivator. That stretches one study of motivation at work. [C-13]
What research shows: tracking your progress works. Across 138 experiments, people who monitored their progress were more likely to reach their goals, and the effect was bigger when they wrote it down or shared it. [S-03]

[VISUAL: a simple weekly tracker with check marks]

## Part 4: The verdict (5:30)

[VISUAL: two columns, "Read it if" and "Skip it if"]

Is Essentialism worth reading? Yes, for its tools. [5A.3]

Read it if other people's requests run your calendar, or your team juggles too many priorities.

Skip it if you already use a strict prioritization system, or if your role gives you little control over what you take on, since the book assumes you can say no. [5A.2]

[VISUAL: three icons: hourglass, if-then note, tracker]

If you do just three things this week: add buffer to your estimates, write one if-then plan for saying no, and track your progress on your top priority. [C-12, S-01, S-03]
