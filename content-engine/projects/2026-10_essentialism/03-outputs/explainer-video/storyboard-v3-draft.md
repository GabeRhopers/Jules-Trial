# Essentialism v3: storyboard draft, about 4 min 30 s (for approval, nothing rendered)

## What changes from v2

| Area | v2 (rendered) | v3 draft |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 75 s | About 4 min 30 s (word count and timing at the end) |
| Depth | 4 one-line tips | 6 takeaways, each = finding + how to use it this week + an example for a busy manager |
| Book summary | 2 ideas, 3 words each | Author and origin, then 2 big ideas with an example per point |
| Honesty | Short verdicts | Same short verdicts, plus one 15-second "take with a grain of salt" scene |
| Illustrations | 1 whiteboard scene | 9 hand-drawn whiteboard scenes (icons, a flowchart, a bar comparison, a timeline, a checklist), alternating with dark cards |
| Design | Stats wrap, small text | Short stats, one idea per screen, headlines of 6 words or fewer, chapter titles between sections |

Claim IDs: [C-xx] = book claim, [S-xx] = research that replaces a weak claim (`01-research/claims-table.md`).
*(practical tip)* = our advice built on the evidence, not a research finding.

## Part 1: The book (0:00 to 1:20)

### 1. Hook · dark card
- **Voice:** "Busy all day, and nothing that matters moved? One book says the fix is doing fewer things, on purpose. We checked its advice against the research, and kept six things you can do this week."
- **On screen:** kicker "Book analysis · Essentialism" / headline "Do fewer things. Which tips work?"

### 2. The problem · whiteboard illustration
- **Voice:** "The book is Essentialism, by Greg McKeown, from 2014. His diagnosis: we spread our effort across too many things, so nothing moves much, and other people's priorities fill our days. His answer: less, but better."
- **Drawing:** a calendar, then arrows shooting in every direction ("too many yeses"), then one long arrow ("less, but better"), circled.
- **On screen (small, under the drawing):** "Greg McKeown · 2014 · began as a 2012 HBR article"
- **Trace:** Source Brief thesis; Source Card

### 3. Big idea 1: the mindset · whiteboard, 3 icons in a row
- **Voice:** "The book has two big ideas. The first is a mindset, in three parts. One: you always have a choice. Choosing is something you do, not something you have. Two: only a few things are vital. Quality pioneer Joseph Juran saw that fixing a small share of problems improves quality enormously. Three: every yes is a trade-off. So ask: which problem do I want?"
- **Drawing:** hand pointer "Choose" · filter "Few are vital" · scale "Trade-off", each drawn as it's spoken
- **Trace:** Ch. 2 to 4, C-02

### 4. Big idea 2: the method · whiteboard, 3-step flow
- **Voice:** "The second idea is a three-step method. Explore: make space to think, and protect your sleep. He points to a study where top violin students slept about 8.6 hours a day. Eliminate: get clear on one main goal, say a graceful no, and cut your losses. Execute: build in buffer time and routines, so the important work happens almost on autopilot."
- **Drawing:** magnifier "Explore" (small moon under it) → scissors "Eliminate" → repeat arrows "Execute"
- **Trace:** Source Brief P2 to P4, C-04

## Part 2: Six takeaways backed by research (1:20 to 3:55)

### 5. How we checked · dark card
- **Voice:** "We checked fourteen claims against meta-analyses and the original studies. Here are six that hold up, or have a better-backed version."
- **On screen:** "14 claims checked · 6 takeaways"
- **Trace:** Claims Table

### 6. Takeaway 1: plan from your worst case · whiteboard bars
- **Voice:** "One: plan from your worst case. In a classic study, students guessed they'd finish their thesis in 34 days. They took 55. Even their worst-case guess, 49 days, was too short, and fewer than a third finished when they said they would. So when you plan a project, start from your worst-case estimate, not your best guess, and still leave room."
- **Drawing:** three hand-drawn bars: "Best guess 34" · "Worst case 49" · "Actual 55" (actual circled)
- **Trace:** C-12 (33.9, 48.6, 55.5 days; 29.7% on time). "Still leave room" = *(practical tip)*

### 7. Takeaway 2: decide your no in advance · whiteboard flowchart
- **Voice:** "Two: decide your no in advance. We found no study testing the book's 90 Percent Rule. What has strong evidence is if-then planning. Across 94 studies, if-then plans helped people start, stay on track, and even drop commitments that were failing. Try this one: if a new request lands, then I ask what it replaces. And when the answer is no, the book's softest version works: no, but here's what I can do."
- **Drawing:** flowchart "New request" → diamond "Serves my top goal?" → yes: "Take it" · no: "Ask what it replaces" (highlighted) → "No, but…"
- **Trace:** C-08 → S-01; "no, but" = book's soft no (Ch. 11, secondhand). Flowchart = *(practical tip)*

### 8. Takeaway 3: cut your losses · dark card with stat
- **Voice:** "Three: cut your losses. We keep feeding losing projects because of what we've already spent. A meta-analysis of 98 results confirms it, and training in economics didn't protect people. The book's test works here: if I weren't already in this, would I sign up for it today?"
- **On screen:** "Cut your losses" / stat "98 results confirm it" / proof "Sunk-cost meta-analysis, 2015"
- **Trace:** C-10; ownership test (Ch. 12), reworded

### 9. Takeaway 4: track your progress · whiteboard notebook
- **Voice:** "Four: track your progress. The book says small wins are the most powerful motivator. That stretches one study. What holds up: across 138 experiments, people who tracked their progress reached more of their goals, especially when they wrote it down or shared it. Try one line every Friday: what moved on my one priority this week?"
- **Drawing:** notebook with three check marks, a pencil, then a "share" arrow
- **Trace:** C-13 → S-03. "Every Friday" = *(practical tip)*

### 10. Takeaway 5: protect your attention · whiteboard, moon and coffee cup
- **Voice:** "Five: protect your attention. Losing sleep hits attention and working memory hardest, so guard your sleep before a demanding week. The book also praises play. For adults, that evidence is thin. What does help: short breaks restore energy and reduce fatigue. After demanding work, you may need more than ten minutes."
- **Drawing:** moon "Sleep: attention" · cup "Breaks: energy" · clock "10+ min after hard work"
- **Trace:** C-06 (Lim & Dinges 2010); C-07 → S-02 (Albulescu et al. 2022)

### 11. Takeaway 6: give routines two months · whiteboard timeline
- **Voice:** "Six: give routines time. The book is right that routine makes the essential automatic. But in the best-known study, it took a median of 66 days, not the 21 you often hear. So pick one routine for your top priority and give it two months."
- **Drawing:** a calendar timeline with marks at "21 days (myth)", crossed out, and "66 days (median)", circled
- **Trace:** C-14 (Lally et al. 2010). "Two months" = *(practical tip)* from the median
