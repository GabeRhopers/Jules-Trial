## Part 2: Three tools that hold up (1:25 to 3:20)

### 5. How we checked · dark card

### 6. Core tool 1: estimate from your past, not your plan · whiteboard bars
- **Voice:** "Tool one: estimate from your past, not your plan. The book is right that we underestimate how long things take. Students guessed they'd finish their thesis in 34 days. They took 55. Even their worst-case guess was too short. Why? We picture the plan going right, and forget how past projects went. In the same research, when people were asked to connect their estimate to similar past tasks, the optimism disappeared. So before you commit to a deadline, look up how long your last two or three similar projects actually took, and start from that."
- **Drawing:** three bars "Best guess 34" · "Worst case 49" · "Actual 55" (actual circled), then a small "past projects" folder icon with an arrow into the estimate
- **Trace:** C-12 (Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994: 33.9, 48.6, 55.5 days; Study 4: bias eliminated when connecting relevant past experiences). "Last two or three projects" = *(practical tip)*

### 7. Core tool 2: write if-then rules · whiteboard flowchart
- **Voice:** "Tool two: write if-then rules. We found no study testing the 90 Percent Rule. But a related idea has strong evidence: if-then plans. You name a specific situation, and the exact action you'll take. Across 94 studies, these plans helped people start on goals, stay on track, and even drop commitments that were failing. The trick is deciding before the moment arrives. For example: if a new request lands, then I ask what it replaces. If it doesn't serve my top goal, then I offer what I can do instead."
- **Drawing:** flowchart "New request" → diamond "Serves my top goal?" → yes: "Take it" · no: "Offer an alternative" (highlighted); above it, the template "IF (situation) → THEN (action)"
- **Trace:** C-08 → S-01 (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). The two example rules = *(practical tip)*

### 8. Core tool 3: track progress in writing · whiteboard notebook
- **Voice:** "Tool three: track your progress, in writing. The book says small wins are the most powerful motivator. That stretches one study. Tracking holds up. Across 138 experiments, getting people to monitor their progress helped them reach more of their goals, and the effect was bigger when progress was written down or shared. Tracking shows you the gap between where you are and where you meant to be. Try one line every Friday: what moved on my one priority this week? Then share it with one person."
- **Drawing:** notebook with a "Friday" line and three check marks, a pencil, then a "share" arrow to a person icon
- **Trace:** C-13 → S-03 (Harkin et al. 2016). "Gap" = the review's rationale (monitoring between setting and attaining a goal). "Friday line" = *(practical tip)*

# Full analysis behind the video (not in the description)

## Strengths of the book
- The execution chapters rest on solid science: the planning fallacy (C-12), sunk-cost bias (C-10, meta-analysis) and habit formation (C-14).
- The sleep argument holds up: the violin figures match the original study (C-04), and a meta-analysis shows sleep loss hurts attention and working memory (C-06).
- Practical tools: decision questions, scripts for saying no, buffer time.

## Weaknesses and blind spots
- The core framing (clarity paradox, 90 Percent Rule, team clarity) is the author's experience, not research (C-01, C-08, C-09).
- Some cited science has moved on: learned helplessness revised (C-03), deliberate practice not replicated (C-05), endowment effect debated (C-11).
- Some findings are stretched: small wins (C-13), adult play (C-07).
- Reader reviews note the advice assumes you can say no, and that parts repeat the author's HBR writing (opinion, not tested).
